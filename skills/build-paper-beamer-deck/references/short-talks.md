# Short research talks

## Decide what listeners should remember

A short talk is a selection of the paper's argument, not a compressed walkthrough of every section. Before slide creation, write the research question, central causal insight, strongest relevant evidence, and the one scope boundary that changes how the evidence should be understood.

Choose visuals after this reading. A paper's first or most elaborate diagram may not be its best short-talk visual. Inspect labels and panel logic before reuse. Do not reproduce mistakes in a source figure as newly authored claims.

## 90-second teaser

A useful starting point is three substantive slides:

1. A concrete problem or surprising question, with paper identity and authors integrated where readable.
2. The key insight and enough mechanism to make it believable.
3. One or two clearly named results, evaluation context, and an invitation to explore the paper.

A requested formal title slide takes precedence over the integrated-opening example. Include the title, official logo, authors, and affiliations without distorting or redrawing the logo, and count the cover in the total slide budget.

This is an option, not a universal slide limit. Follow the actual conference instructions supplied by the user. Do not invent video resolution, aspect ratio, file-size, or deadline requirements when they are not provided.

Budget approximately 160–190 English words for a measured 90-second narration, including transitions and pauses. Numerical results and unfamiliar terms take longer to say than to skim. Spell numbers out in the spoken script when this improves timing estimates. Plan slide holds whose total matches the requested duration, then recommend rehearsal. Do not claim a slide deck itself satisfies a video submission requirement.

## Two- to three-minute PhD introduction

Honor a maximum of two or three slides as a total count, including any cover. One useful three-slide structure is:

1. The research question and why it matters, with the speaker's identity integrated.
2. One representative project and an intuitive explanation of the approach.
3. Evidence and the next research question, tied back to the broader aim.

Use accessible language before acronyms or architecture details. Avoid a publication list, exhaustive taxonomy, and a standalone title or thank-you page that consumes the slide budget.

Ask for the broader PhD topic when it cannot be inferred confidently. If proceeding with a paper-based provisional framing, say so in the handoff and keep it easy to revise. Never invent completed projects, authorship roles, future commitments, or a formal thesis title. Describe joint paper methods as joint work unless the user's individual contribution is known.

For a target around 2:30, roughly 280–330 English words at an explicitly stated pace is a useful starting point. Allocate more time to the example than the identity line. The script should explain relationships; it should not simply read every slide label aloud.

## Captions, evidence, and notes

- Keep the visible Figure number and a concise, meaningful caption. Archive the full source caption in notes or a companion source map.
- Preserve qualifiers such as label accuracy versus exact-match accuracy, laboratory platform versus field deployment, and relative waveform versus absolute displacement.
- Keep subtitles, data labels, and explanatory text large enough for a recorded presentation. Avoid long source paragraphs on the slide.
- Put rehearsal timings, spoken narration, full citations, and detailed scope notes in speaker notes or a companion script.
- Reuse the same paper figures across independently requested versions when they support each version's narrative.
